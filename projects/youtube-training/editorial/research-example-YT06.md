# Research example — YT06, the 10% brain myth

This is the standard the other nine dossiers are held to, shown end to end: what
was checked, what the sources actually say, and one claim that was **rejected
and kept on the record**. Researcher: scipio, 2026-10-15. Every line below was
checked against the named source on that date; anything that could not be
checked is labelled as such instead of being smoothed over.

## Method

1. Find the earliest appearance of the claim, not the most popular retelling.
2. Compare each retelling against the previous one and name what was *added*.
3. Check the debunking against the scientific literature, not against another
   popular article.
4. Actively hunt the version that would be most embarrassing to get wrong (the
   Einstein quote) and record what the archive says.

## Checked passages

**Hop 1 — William James (primary, public domain).**
Open copy: *The Energies of Men*, New York: Moffat, Yard and Company, 1908 —
full scanned text, Internet Archive, item `energiesofmen00jame`
(https://archive.org/details/energiesofmen00jame). The sentence the myth is
built on, in James's own framing:

> "…we are making use of only a small part of our possible mental and physical
> resources."

What is **not** in James: any percentage, any mention of brain tissue, any claim
about neurology. He is writing about effort and habit. Verified in the open
1908 text; the exact page number must be cited from the scan on final-export day
(recorded as an open item, not glossed).

**Hop 2 — Lowell Thomas's foreword (1936).**
Thomas wrote the foreword to Dale Carnegie's *How to Win Friends and Influence
People* (1936) and attributed to James a claim James never made. The foreword's
line, as quoted across the scholarly accounts:

> "Professor William James of Harvard used to say that the average man develops
> only 10 percent of his latent mental ability."

Checked against two independent secondary accounts (Beyerstein's Scientific
American column and Wired's 2014 summary), which quote the foreword
consistently. Open item for production: obtain a scan of the 1936 foreword
itself to quote verbatim on screen; until then the film attributes the line to
Thomas "as widely quoted" and shows the secondary sources.

**The scholarly account of the lineage.**
Barry L. Beyerstein, "Do We Really Use Only 10 Percent of the Brain?",
Scientific American (Ask the Brains), 2004 —
https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/do-we-really-use-only-10/ — checked
2026-10-15. Key verified statements:

> "I was never able to find an exact percentage mentioned, and James always
> talked in terms of one's undeveloped potential, apparently never relating this
> to a specific amount of gray matter engaged."

> "…the biggest boost for the self-help entrepreneurs came when the famous
> adventurer and journalist Lowell Thomas attributed the 10-percent-of-the-brain
> claim to William James."

Beyerstein also gives the "silent cortex" hop: early investigators' technical
term for association areas was misread publicly as "brain with no function."

## The rejected claim (kept on the record)

**Claim:** *"Einstein said the 10% figure, or attributed his own genius to using
more of his brain."*

**Status: REJECTED — do not state as fact; use only as the film's example of an
apocryphal quote.**

Evidence for the rejection: staff at the Einstein Archives (Caltech) searched
the record in 2004 and found **no evidence Einstein ever made such a statement**
(reported in Beyerstein's account and corroborated by Wired's summary). The
quote circulates in self-help literature with no primary source in any edition.
Registry entry: `slate-registry.json → rejected[0]`, so the refusal stays
visible even if this file is skimmed.

Why we keep it: the film's fourth act is *how* attributions get invented. Cutting
the Einstein quote would remove the best evidence for the film's own thesis.

## Secondary checks that did NOT survive

- Several popular pages place the James essay in "1906" and others in "1907/08".
  The essay was delivered as an address in 1906–07 and published as a book in
  1908. The film will say "around 1907" and cite the 1908 book scan — precise
  enough to be true, honest about the spread.
- One widely repeated framing ("early researchers could only find functions for
  10% of the brain") is itself a *retelling* of the silent-cortex error, not a
  separate historical fact. The film uses it only inside the myth-lineage
  timeline, never as an independent claim.
