# YT03 — The Day of Two Noons: How Railroads Invented Your Time Zone

**Format:** mini-documentary (V07 card `t-20261015-8e11`) · 300–330 s · slot YT03

## Audience and promise

A viewer who has never questioned why their phone knows the time. Promise:
**the reason your clock agrees with a stranger's clock a thousand miles away was
a decision made by railroad companies on one November Sunday in 1883 — and
governments only caught up decades later.** The surprise is who was in charge.

## Sources (three, one primary)

1. **Primary:** William F. Allen, *Standard Time: with a Map Showing the Time
   Meridians Adopted by the Railway Convention Held in Chicago, Oct. 11, 1883,
   and a Brief Sketch of the History of the Subject* (Travellers' Official
   Guide / New York, 1883) — the proposal itself, in the public domain and held
   in several open digital collections.
2. U.S. National Archives / NOAA record of the 1883 adoption and the 1918
   Standard Time Act (15 U.S.C. ch. 6 origin) — the legal catch-up.
   https://www.archives.gov and https://www.congress.gov (act text)
3. Smithsonian / Allegheny Observatory records on the time-service telegraph
   signal used by railroads — the mechanism by which the reset was coordinated.

Link checking is part of the production card: any source that cannot be opened
on the render day gets replaced before the claim it supports is spoken.

Uncertainty is marked: contemporaneous local reactions vary by town, and the
film attributes each quoted reaction to a named town rather than generalising.
The "35-year gap" is stated as 1883→1918, not as a claim about every state.

## Rights / asset strategy

All maps, timetables and diagrams are original animations built from public-domain
source material. No archival footage is used, so nothing needs a licence fee or
an archive agreement. If a public-domain photograph is used for texture, its
holding institution and access date are logged in the shot's asset record.
