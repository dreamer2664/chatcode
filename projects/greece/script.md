## ACT I — The coast of revolt (Ionia and Marathon)

In the year 499 before the common era, the Greek cities of the Ionian
coast — today the western edge of Turkey — rose against the Persian kings
who owned them [Hdt 5.28–38]. They burned Sardis, the great inland capital
[Hdt 5.100–102], and the fire was never forgiven. Darius, king of kings,
swore to remember Athens, because Athens had lent the rebels a few ships
[Hdt 5.97].

Ten years later he collected the debt. A Persian fleet landed at the bay
of Marathon, north of Athens [Hdt 6.102–117]. The Athenians, a few thousand
citizen-soldiers, walked out to meet it with a small band of Plataeans —
the only city that came [Hdt 6.108]. Herodotus says they attacked at a run,
the first Greeks ever to charge at a sprint [Hdt 6.112]. What followed was
shocking: some six thousand four hundred Persians dead against one hundred
and ninety-two Athenians [Hdt 6.117].

The famous runner of Marathon — the man who sprints to Athens and dies —
is not in Herodotus. The runner Herodotus does give us is Pheidippides, who
ran from Athens to Sparta in two days to ask for help [Hdt 6.105–106].
Where the facts are better than the legend, we keep the facts.

Herodotus gives us one perfect image of the cause: Darius, hearing that
Sardis had burned, asked who the Athenians were, and then demanded an
archer's bow, and kept a servant to whisper to him at every meal, Master,
remember the Athenians [Hdt 5.105]. The empire had a long memory, and a
short temper.

## ACT II — The narrow place (Thermopylae and Salamis)

Darius died with the debt unpaid; his son Xerxes came himself, in 480, with
an army the sources insist was too large to count [Hdt 7.20–56]. At the hot
springs of Thermopylae a few thousand Greeks held the narrow road for three
days, until a local man showed the Persians a path around the mountain
[Hdt 7.202–222]. Leonidas and his three hundred Spartans stayed, by law
and by choice, and died there [Hdt 7.222–225].

But the real weapon of that year was a strait. Themistocles, the Athenian
who had convinced his city to build a navy with silver from a new mine
[Hdt 7.144], lured the enormous Persian fleet into the narrow water at
Salamis, where numbers meant nothing [Hdt 8.56–75]. In the crush, the big
fleet beat itself against the rocks and its own oars [Hdt 8.84–96]. A man
who was there, the playwright Aeschylus, put it in a line: the sea was
full of wrecks and drowning men [Aeschylus, Persians 419–428].

Before all this, Athens had asked the oracle at Delphi what to do, and the
oracle answered in riddles about wooden walls and a divine Salamis
[Hdt 7.140–144]. It was Themistocles who read the riddle as ships — and the
city staked its life on his reading [Hdt 7.143–144]. While the pass held at
Thermopylae, the same navy had been bleeding the Persians at Artemisium in
the north [Hdt 8.14–15]. One plan, two narrow places.

## ACT III — The last field (Plataea)

The next year, 479, the Persian general Mardonius tried Greece by land.
At Plataea the Spartan regent Pausanias commanded the largest Greek army
that had ever stood together [Hdt 9.46–64]. When the lines finally locked,
the Persians fought bravely, but armour and long spears won: Mardonius fell
and his camp was stormed [Hdt 9.61–70]. On the same day, says the tradition,
the Greek fleet won at Mycale on the Ionian coast [Hdt 9.100–102] — a
coincidence so neat the ancients themselves marvelled at it. The invasion
was over. The small cities had held.

## ACT IV — The school of Hellas (Delian League and Pericles)

Victory organised itself. In 478 the island cities and Athens founded a
league against Persia, its treasury on the sacred island of Delos
[Thuc 1.96]. Year by year the league became an empire, and the treasury
moved to Athens — around 454, though the exact date is read from stone and
argued [IG I³ 71]. With the money came the buildings. Pericles, the cold-
voiced aristocrat the people kept re-electing, raised the Parthenon on the
ruins of the war [Plutarch, Pericles 13].

In the funeral speech Thucydides gives him, Pericles calls Athens the
school of Hellas [Thuc 2.41]. It is the best single line the fifth century
wrote about itself. It is also the speech of a man asking the living to pay
for the dead. We hold both.

Under the same men, Athens walled itself to the sea with the Long Walls, so
that the city could never again be starved off the land [Thuc 1.107–108].
Empire, marble, walls, and a democracy that still excluded women, slaves,
and most of the people it ruled over. The school of Hellas taught every one
of these lessons at once.

Even its safety valve was dramatic: once a year the citizens could scratch
a name on a potsherd and banish a man for ten years — ostracism, the demos
keeping its tallest heads level [Aristotle, Ath. Pol. 22]. Thousands of the
shards survive; the practice is archaeology, not gossip.

## ACT V — The long war (Peloponnesian war)

Sparta watched the school become an empire. The truest cause of the war
that followed, says Thucydides, was fear: the growth of Athenian power and
the alarm it caused in Sparta [Thuc 1.23]. From 431 the Greek world ate
itself for twenty-seven years. Midway, at the small island of Melos,
Athenian envoys argued that the strong do what they can and the weak
suffer what they must [Thuc 5.89]; the Melians who refused to submit were
killed or enslaved [Thuc 5.116]. Thucydides places the dialogue in his book
with almost no comment. The comment is the placement.

A plague entered the crowded city in the second year and took perhaps a
third of it, including Pericles [Thuc 2.47–54, 65]. Then the empire gambled:
a great fleet sent to conquer Sicily, and destroyed at Syracuse in 413
[Thuc 7.71–87], with the cleverest man in Athens, Alcibiades, having fled
to the enemy side [Thuc 6.88–89]. The end came at Aegospotami in 405, when
the Spartan admiral Lysander caught the Athenian fleet beached and cut it
to pieces [Xenophon, Hell. 2.2]. In 404 the walls came down to the sound of
flute-girls, and the empire was a memory [Xenophon, Hell. 2.2].

## ACT VI — The north inherits (Philip and Alexander)

The cities kept fighting over the pieces until the north settled it.
Philip of Macedon, a hostage who had studied Greece as a student of its
wars, built the army that ended Greek freedom at Chaeronea in 338
[Diodorus 16.86]. His son, twenty when Philip was murdered, took that army
east.

In four years Alexander crossed the Granicus [Arrian, Anab. 1.14], broke
Darius at Issus [Arrian, Anab. 2.10], and unmade the Persian empire at
Gaugamela [Arrian, Anab. 3.11]. He burned Persepolis — policy or drunken
dare, the sources disagree [Arrian 3.18; Plutarch, Alex. 37] — and died in
Babylon in 323, at thirty-two, with no heir and no will the sources trust
[Arrian, Anab. 7.26]. His generals carved the corpse into kingdoms, and the
Greek language became the air of the eastern Mediterranean for a thousand
years. He founded Alexandria on the way, a city that would outlive every kingdom
of his successors [Arrian, Anab. 3.1–2]; in that city his general Ptolemy's
house would gather the library the ancient world is always imagined reading.
That is the strange ledger: the
cities that could not share power gave the world its grammar.
